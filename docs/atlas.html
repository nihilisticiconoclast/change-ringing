I · Territory

Foundries had catchment areas, and you can see them

Each dot is a tower, placed by its own coordinates and coloured by the foundry tradition that cast most of its bells. All 12,428 towers are shown in one colour to begin with, because eight hues scattered across a map is unreadable — select a tradition and it lights up against the rest. Then drag the slider back through the centuries.

2026
Rudhall of Gloucester is the clearest case: a dense cluster through Gloucestershire, Herefordshire and the Welsh marches, and almost nothing beyond it. The firm cast 2,745 surviving bells between 1679 and 1835 without ever becoming national. Loughborough and Whitechapel, by contrast, are everywhere — the railways let them be.

II · Succession

Two foundries outlived everyone else

Surviving bells by quarter-century and tradition. This counts bells still hanging, not bells cast — losses, recastings and wartime scrap fall out of the record, so the deep past is thinner than it truly was.

III · The cross-corpus join

Where new methods get rung first

This is the question that needs all three sources at once: Dove's founder records, joined to bells, joined to towers, joined to the CCCBR Methods Library's first-performance history. It asks whose bells ringers chose when trying something that had never been rung before.

Loughborough's 8,306 is partly its own doing: John Taylor & Co's Bell Foundry Tower in Loughborough is itself the single most-used tower for method first-peals, at 507. A foundry that keeps a ring on site gets to host the experiments.

IV · The traditions

Eight houses

Dove groups individual firms into foundry traditions, which is what makes this legible: “Loughborough” absorbs thirteen successive businesses, from John Taylor's arrival in 1786 to the present day.
